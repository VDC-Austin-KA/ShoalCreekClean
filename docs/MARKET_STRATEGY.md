# Shoal Creek Clean: Market Strategy

Status: working draft for the three founding partners
Scope: exterior cleaning (power washing, soft washing, gutters, surface sealing) in Austin, Round Rock, Pflugerville, Cedar Park, and greater Central Texas

---

## 1. Executive Summary

Shoal Creek Clean (SCC) is a commercial-first exterior cleaning company for the Greater Austin metro. We launch with commercial power washing because it carries larger tickets, recurring demand, and buyers who value reliable scheduling over the lowest price. Residential work is limited in Phase 1 to driveways and patios, priced in the same engine for consistency.

Our wedge against established regional vendors and franchise operators is operational:

- **Speed.** A written quote within 24 hours of a complete site request, and an instant online estimator for simple jobs.
- **Scheduling.** Automated booking, reminders, and crew routing, so property managers stop chasing vendors.
- **Proof.** Timestamped before-and-after photos for every job, stored in a client portal and attached to the work order.

Owner-operators offer personal attention but rarely have systems. Franchise operators have brand and capital but often quote slowly and deliver inconsistent crew quality. We aim to match the owner-operator's responsiveness with the franchise operator's documentation.

Phase 2 (siding and soft wash, commercial gutters, surface sealing, and preventive maintenance contracts) starts only when Phase 1 hits defined revenue and crew milestones (Section 3.2).

Launch-year target: 60 to 80 active commercial accounts across the metro by Month 12, blended gross margin of 55 to 65 percent, and at least 40 percent of revenue under recurring schedules by Month 18.

### 2.2 Target segments

Each profile lists the buyer persona, pain points, decision maker, typical contract, and sales cycle.

**A. Commercial property managers (third-party management firms)**
- Persona: portfolio manager for 5 to 40 properties, often run from outside the market, measured on NOI and tenant complaints.
- Pain points: no-show vendors, unclear invoices, no documentation when a tenant complains.
- Decision maker: regional or portfolio manager; accounts payable signs off.
- Contract: master service agreement with per-property work orders; Net 30; annual rate review.
- Sales cycle: 4 to 10 weeks, including a vendor packet (COI, W-9, safety summary).

**B. HOAs and condominium associations**
- Persona: volunteer board member or community association manager (CAM) with a fixed budget.
- Pain points: inconsistent curb appeal, resident complaints about entrances and clubhouses, emergency work that overruns budget.
- Decision maker: board president or treasurer; CAM handles day-to-day. Board vote needed above the bylaw threshold.
- Contract: annual scope (entrances, pool decks, clubhouse, walkways) billed quarterly or monthly.
- Sales cycle: 6 to 12 weeks, paced by board meetings. Plan for two presentations.

**C. Retail centers and strip malls**
- Persona: asset manager or leasing director responsible for common-area appearance and tenant retention.
- Pain points: grease at dumpster enclosures, sidewalk staining, parking-lot grime, algae and oil slip hazards.
- Decision maker: center property manager; asset manager approves above a cap.
- Contract: scope per center (sidewalks, drive aisles, dumpster pads, storefront fronts) on a quarterly or semi-annual cycle, plus an emergency callout rate.
- Sales cycle: 3 to 8 weeks, often triggered by a tenant complaint or lease renewal.

**D. Restaurants (drive-thrus, dumpster pads, grease)**
- Persona: owner-operator, or franchisee operations director for multi-unit groups.
- Pain points: grease on drive-thru lanes and pickup windows, dumpster pad contamination, pests, inspection risk.
- Decision maker: owner (single unit) or franchisee ops director (multi-unit). Franchisor standards can restrict vendor choice.
- Contract: monthly or bi-monthly after-hours service; single units often start with a one-time deep clean.
- Sales cycle: 1 to 4 weeks for owner-operators; 2 to 4 months for multi-unit groups.
- Note: most restaurant work carries the +15 percent after-hours commercial surcharge. Frame it as the cost of avoiding service disruption.

**E. Single storefronts**
- Persona: small business owner (salon, medical office, bank branch, auto service, boutique).
- Pain points: window and facade grime, sidewalk staining, fast turnaround before an opening.
- Decision maker: the owner; a single-signature purchase.
- Contract: one-time facade or sidewalk clean, with an option for quarterly maintenance.
- Sales cycle: same day to 2 weeks. Responds well to door-to-door, referrals, and Google reviews.

**F. Office parks**
- Persona: property manager for a multi-building campus, or a tenant's facilities lead.
- Pain points: entry plaza and walkway staining, parking-structure grime, facade streaking that hurts leasing.
- Decision maker: property manager; sometimes a corporate real estate group.
- Contract: annual or semi-annual scope with a defined building list; often bundled with building soft wash in Phase 2.
- Sales cycle: 6 to 12 weeks, with heavier insurance and safety paperwork.

**G. Multifamily (apartment communities)**
- Persona: on-site community manager and off-site regional manager.
- Pain points: move-in appearance, pool deck and breezeway grime, trash enclosure areas, resident complaints.
- Decision maker: regional manager approves; community manager signs off on completion.
- Contract: community-wide scope, typically quarterly, with move-in and turnover add-ons.
- Sales cycle: 4 to 8 weeks; scheduling must avoid resident hours and pool closures.

## 3. Phase 2 Roadmap

Phase 2 adds service lines that reuse the same crews, customers, and scheduling system. Each line has an activation gate. We do not start a line because it looks attractive.

### 3.1 Service lines

| Service line | What it adds | Why it fits | Key risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| Siding and soft wash, roof soft wash | Higher-margin, low-pressure work on stucco, vinyl, brick, roofs | Same customers, larger tickets | Chemical handling; roof fall exposure |
| Commercial gutter cleaning | Recurring work for PMs and multifamily | Pairs with building wash on one visit | Height and fall-protection exposure |
| Surface sealing (pavers, concrete) | Periodic high-ticket work on a long cycle | Pairs with flatwork and lot accounts | Weather windows and cure-time scheduling |
| Preventive maintenance contracts | Predictable revenue across all lines | Converts one-time jobs to contracts | Needs pricing discipline and an account manager |

### 3.2 Sequencing gates

Gates are measured on a rolling 90-day basis, not a single month.

| Gate | Trigger | Unlocks |
|---|---|---|
| Gate 0 | Monthly revenue of at least $60,000 for 3 consecutive months; 2 crews booked 6 weeks out | Siding and soft wash pilots |
| Gate 1 | Soft wash pilots at 85 percent or better on-time; zero chemical or runoff incidents in 90 days | Full soft wash and roof soft wash |
| Gate 2 | 15 or more active commercial accounts with a recurring visit; third crew hired | Commercial gutter cleaning |
| Gate 3 | Two months of flatwork margin at 60 percent or better; a lead tech certified by the sealer manufacturer | Surface sealing |
| Gate 4 | 40 percent or more of revenue on recurring schedules for two consecutive quarters | Formal maintenance contract tiers with annual terms |

These are planning targets. Revisit them after six months of actuals.

### 3.3 Equipment by phase

**Phase 1 (commercial power washing)**
- Two or three trailer-mounted hot-water pressure washers (3,500 to 4,000 PSI class) with surface cleaners.
- Water recovery or vacuum system for commercial lots (see Section 9.1).
- Chemical proportioner and degreaser supply for restaurant and dumpster work.
- Lift access for storefront and facade work up to two stories (rented or owned).
- Telematics and routing software; one tablet per crew for work-order completion and photo capture.
- Spill kits, PPE, and a first-aid kit per truck.

**Phase 2 (soft wash and roof soft wash)**
- Low-pressure soft-wash system with dedicated chemical tank, metering pump, and rinse system.
- Extension wands and rinse setup for two-story work.
- Fall-protection equipment (harnesses, lanyards, anchors) and documented training before any roof work.
- Additional lift capacity for three-story contracts.

**Phase 2 (gutter cleaning)**
- Telescoping water-fed poles with a purified-water (DI or RO) rinse for spot-free results.
- Gutter vacuum units; extension ladders and stabilizers with ladder-safety training.

**Phase 2 (surface sealing)**
- Airless or pump sprayers, rollers, and squeegees for controlled application.
- Rotary floor machine or walk-behind surface prep unit, and a blower.
- Sealer storage with temperature and humidity monitoring.

**Phase 2 (maintenance contracts)**
- No new field equipment. Requires contract management features in scheduling software and an account manager role.

### 4.3 Positioning against the market

- Minimum charges protect route efficiency. Jobs that fall below the minimum should be routed to a partner or declined.

## 5. Positioning: Beating Established Regional Vendors

**Speed**
- 24-hour written quote turnaround, measured from the first complete site packet (address, scope, photos, access notes).
- Instant online estimator for flatwork, driveway, and storefront jobs under the auto-quote limits; a price in under two minutes.
- Same-week scheduling for standard work when a crew is available.

**Automated scheduling**
- Online booking tied to crew availability and route density.
- Reminders at 72 and 24 hours, each with a reschedule link.
- Recurring schedules created once and maintained automatically.
- One point of contact per property manager, with a portal for work-order history.

**Visual quality verification**
- Timestamped before-and-after photos for every job, captured on the crew tablet and attached to the work order.
- Client portal showing photo proof, completion notes, water and chemical handling notes, and invoices.
- Target: photo proof on 100 percent of completed jobs. Jobs without proof are flagged before invoicing.

**Where we do not compete:** lowest-price bids on large, low-complexity flatwork; residential work beyond driveways and patios in Phase 1; jobs outside the service area or needing equipment we do not own.

## 6. Unit Economics Sketch

### 6.1 Assumptions

| Assumption | Value | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Crew size | 2 techs | Standard two-person crew |
| Production basis | Per tech-hour | Section 4.2 rates are per tech-hour; a 2-tech crew doubles output |
| Loaded labor cost | $30 per tech-hour | Wage plus payroll tax, workers comp, and benefits allowance (assumed) |
| Chemicals (soft wash) | $0.03 per sq ft | Assumed; surfactant and sodium hypochlorite blend |
| Chemicals (flatwork) | $0.01 per sq ft | Assumed; detergent and degreaser where needed |
| Fuel and water | $0.01 to $0.02 per sq ft | Assumed; truck and trailer fuel, potable water |
| Equipment wear and consumables | $0.01 per sq ft | Assumed; tips, hoses, filters, pump maintenance |
| Insurance (GL, auto, workers comp) | 7 percent of revenue, treated as direct cost | Assumed midpoint of 6 to 9 percent; verify with quotes |
| Target gross margin | 55 to 65 percent, blended | Management target |

### 6.2 Worked examples

**Flatwork at $0.18 per sq ft**
- Revenue per tech-hour: 1,200 sq ft x $0.18 = $216.
- Labor: $30 / 1,200 = $0.025 per sq ft.
- Chemicals $0.010, fuel and water $0.010, equipment $0.010, insurance $0.0126 (7 percent of $0.18).
- Direct cost: $0.0676 per sq ft. Gross margin: about 62 percent.

**Building soft wash at $0.28 per sq ft**
- Revenue per tech-hour: 600 sq ft x $0.28 = $168.
- Labor: $30 / 600 = $0.050 per sq ft.
- Chemicals $0.030, fuel and water $0.015, equipment $0.010, insurance $0.0196.
- Direct cost: $0.1246 per sq ft. Gross margin: about 55 percent, the bottom of the blended target.

Building soft wash sits at the low end of the target at list price, and falls below it on small buildings where the minimum is not reached or travel time is high. Options: raise crew productivity, price small buildings closer to the minimum, and cut chemical waste with better dilution control. Do not assume soft wash will carry the blended margin in Phase 1.

**Gutter cleaning at $1.40 per linear ft**
- Revenue per tech-hour: 120 lf x $1.40 = $168.
- Labor: $30 / 120 = $0.25 per linear ft.
- Equipment, disposal, and consumables: about $0.15 per linear ft (assumed). Insurance: $0.098.
- Direct cost: about $0.50 per linear ft. Gross margin: about 64 percent.

**Flatwork ticket example**
A 1,800 sq ft commercial sidewalk and dumpster pad job is $324 at $0.18 per sq ft (above the $250 minimum). At 1,200 sq ft per tech-hour that is 1.5 tech-hours, or $45 of labor. Chemicals, fuel and water, and equipment add $54; insurance adds about $23. Direct costs total about $122, so gross margin is about 62 percent before overhead.

### 6.3 Implications
- Flatwork and gutters carry the margin. Soft wash is a volume and relationship product in Phase 1.
- Enforce minimum charges on low-productivity jobs.
- Insurance cost tracks payroll and claims. Training and documentation protect the rate.
- Overhead (software, sales, admin, vehicles) sits below gross margin and must be modeled separately.

## 7. 90-Day Go-to-Market Plan

Assumes all three partners are full time and one lead tech is hired before Week 3.

- **Week 1 (foundations):** Confirm entity, bank account, and partner roles (sales, operations, finance). Verify the price book against the engine. Start 10 secret-shop quote calls. Draft the vendor packet.
- **Week 2 (insurance and compliance):** Request quotes for GL, commercial auto, and workers comp. Contact the City of Austin Watershed Protection Department on wash-water rules. Confirm sales-tax treatment with the Texas Comptroller.
- **Week 3 (crew and equipment):** Hire and onboard the lead tech; complete chemical handling and fall-protection training. Acquire two pressure washers and one water recovery setup. Configure scheduling and work orders with photo capture.
- **Week 4 (digital presence):** Launch the website with the instant estimator for flatwork and storefront work. Complete the Google Business Profile for each market. Open the client portal.
- **Week 5 (target list):** Build lists of about 150 property management firms, 60 retail centers, and 80 restaurant groups across the core markets. Map decision makers.
- **Week 6 (outreach starts):** Send 40 cold emails to Austin property managers. Make 25 calls to retail center managers. Start restaurant door-to-door in the Round Rock corridor.
- **Week 7 (first quotes):** Target 10 site visits and 10 written quotes. Track turnaround against the 24-hour standard.
- **Week 8 (pilots):** Offer a first-service discount to 3 pilot accounts in exchange for references and photo case studies. Plan Pflugerville and Cedar Park routes.
- **Week 9 (second wave):** Send cold emails to HOAs and multifamily communities. Start LinkedIn outreach to commercial property managers.
- **Week 10 (contracts):** Convert at least 2 pilots to recurring schedules. Finalize the master service agreement and work-order templates.
- **Week 11 (operations review):** Review crew utilization, job margins, and rework. Correct production-rate assumptions if actuals differ from the price book.
- **Week 12 (referrals):** Ask every pilot for one referral and one review. Launch a credit-on-next-service referral offer for property managers.
- **Week 13 (gate review):** Review KPIs against Section 8. Decide on the second crew, Gate 0 tracking, or pricing changes.

## 9. Risks and Compliance

Items marked **Verify** must be confirmed with the named authority or a qualified professional before we rely on them. Nothing here is legal or tax advice.

### 9.1 Wash-water discharge and stormwater
- **Verify:** City of Austin Watershed Protection Department rules for wash-water discharge on commercial sites, including any controls required on impervious cover and any ordinance on discharge to storm drains.
- **Verify:** TCEQ requirements that may apply to wash water, including general permit coverage and stormwater pollution prevention expectations.
- **Recommendation:** On commercial lots, use water reclamation or vacuum recovery, with berms or inlet protection where the site allows. Record the controls on each work order and in the photo record.
- **Recommendation:** No degreasers or chemicals near storm inlets without controls. SDS sheets on every truck.

### 9.2 Insurance
- **Recommendation:** General liability, commercial auto, and workers comp at launch. Most commercial clients require a certificate of insurance (COI) naming them as additional insured before work starts.
- **Verify:** Texas workers comp rules. Texas lets many private employers opt out of workers comp, but commercial clients and their insurers often require coverage. Confirm the approach with a licensed insurance broker and an attorney.
- **Verify:** Pollution liability coverage for chemical and runoff exposure, and its exclusions.
- **Recommendation:** Review coverage annually and after any claim.

### 9.3 Sales tax
- **Verify:** With the Texas Comptroller of Public Accounts, whether each service line (building and flatwork cleaning, gutter cleaning, sealing) is taxable, and under what conditions.
- **Verify:** Tax treatment of materials and of services billed to commercial versus residential customers.
- **Recommendation:** Configure tax treatment by service line in invoicing before the first invoice.

### 9.4 Worker safety and OSHA
- **Verify:** Which OSHA standards apply to our height work (roof soft wash, ladder work, lift work). Coverage can differ between general industry and construction scopes.
- **Recommendation:** Written fall-protection plan before any roof or elevated work, with harnesses, anchors, and training documented.
- **Recommendation:** Hazard communication program for chemicals (SDS on site, labeling, training) and chemical-handling PPE.
- **Recommendation:** Vehicle and equipment training, including high-pressure injection injury awareness.

### 9.5 Other operational risks
- **Chemical storage:** Verify local and state rules for oxidizers such as sodium hypochlorite. Use secondary containment.
- **Property damage:** Photograph pre-existing conditions before work starts. Set pressures to avoid damage to stucco, wood, and sealed surfaces.
- **HOA and municipal rules:** Some communities require notice or approval for certain work. Verify before scheduling.
- **Seasonality:** Summer heat and winter freezes affect scheduling (see Section 10).

## 10. Seasonal Demand Notes

- **Feb to Apr:** Pollen and tree debris peak. Flatwork, storefront, and sidewalk demand rises.
- **May to Aug:** Heat limits daytime work. Shift commercial and restaurant jobs to early morning or after hours; expect the after-hours surcharge.
- **Sept to Oct:** Post-summer grime and mildew. Good window for building soft wash and gutters before winter.
- **Nov to Dec:** Holiday storefront preparation; short-window demand for facades, entrances, and sidewalks.
